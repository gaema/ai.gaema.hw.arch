arch · gaema

Thirteen AI accelerators, drawn the same way

Every vendor names its parts differently — a compute unit, an Xe-core, a streaming multiprocessor, a Tensix tile — and every vendor draws its own block diagram at its own level of detail. These thirteen are drawn to one structure, so you can click from the card down to the thing that actually does the multiply and compare like with like. Ten of them carry memory of their own. The other three share the machine's: two GPU blocks living inside a CPU package and one superchip, each reading the same pool its own CPU uses — and that difference is visible on their maps.

AMD

Radeon AI PRO R9700

RDNA 4 · Navi 48

AMD's first RDNA 4 professional card: 64 compute units, 128 second-generation AI accelerators, and 32 GB of GDDR6 behind a 64 MB Infinity Cache.

 AMD

Radeon 610M (gfx1036)

RDNA 2 (integrated) · gfx1036 graphics block

The two-compute-unit RDNA 2 block inside the Ryzen Raphael / Granite Ridge packages: 128 stream processors, 256 KiB of L2, no matrix engine at all — RDNA 2 predates WMMA, so the closest thing to one is the vector dot4 instruction.

 Intel

Arc Pro B50

Xe2 (Battlemage) · BMG-G21

The small half of the Battlemage professional line: 16 Xe-cores and 16 GB of GDDR6 inside 70 W, on a half-height card with no power connector at all — the same Xe-core as the B70, at half the width.

 Intel

Arc Pro B70

Xe2 (Battlemage) · BMG-G31

The largest discrete Xe2 part: 32 Xe-cores, 256 XMX engines and 32 GB of GDDR6, at 367 INT8 TOPS.

 Intel

Arc A370M

Xe-HPG (Alchemist) · DG2-128

The smallest first-generation Arc: 8 Xe-cores and 4 GB of GDDR6 across a 64-bit bus — the same DPAS matrix path as the bigger Alchemist cards, at an eighth of the memory width.

 Intel

Arc Graphics 140V

Xe2-LPG (Lunar Lake) · Lunar Lake compute tile

The Xe2 graphics block inside Lunar Lake: eight Xe2 cores with the same XMX matrix surface as the discrete Battlemage cards, reading the LPDDR5X soldered into the package — memory shared with the CPU, not a frame buffer.

 NVIDIA

RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell

Blackwell · GB202

The largest shipping GB202: 188 of 192 SMs enabled, 24,064 CUDA cores, 752 fifth-generation Tensor Cores, and 96 GB of GDDR7.

 NVIDIA

GeForce RTX 5090

Blackwell · GB202

The consumer GB202: 170 of 192 SMs, 21,760 CUDA cores, and 32 GB of GDDR7 on the same 512-bit bus as its workstation sibling.

 NVIDIA

DGX Spark

Grace Blackwell · GB10

Two 3 nm dies on one package — twenty Arm cores and a 48-SM Blackwell GPU — reading the same 128 GB of LPDDR5X.

 Tenstorrent

Wormhole n150d

Wormhole · Wormhole ASIC

A grid of 72 independent Tensix tiles, each with five RISC-V cores and 1.5 MB of software-managed SRAM — no warp scheduler, no cache hierarchy, and two QSFP-DD ports plus two Warp 100 bridges to bolt cards together.

 Tenstorrent

Wormhole n300d

Wormhole · 2 × Wormhole ASIC

Two Wormhole ASICs on one actively cooled board — 128 Tensix tiles and 24 GB of GDDR6 at 300 W — joined by two Ethernet links routed across the PCB, not by a shared NOC.

 Tenstorrent

Blackhole p150a

Blackhole · Blackhole ASIC

A grid of 120 independent Tensix tiles, each with five RISC-V cores and 1.5 MB of software-managed SRAM — no warp scheduler, no cache hierarchy, and four QSFP-DD ports to bolt cards together.

 Tenstorrent

Blackhole p300c

Blackhole · 2 × Blackhole ASIC

Two Blackhole ASICs on one liquid-cooled board — 240 Tensix tiles and 64 GB of GDDR6 at 600 W — joined by two Ethernet links routed across the PCB, not by a shared NOC.

Cross-vendor matrix

The same ten questions asked of each part. A cell reads “—” where the figure is unknown, rather than an estimate.

Three of the row labels are links: the rows whose answer is a RATE that more than one vendor publishes open a page comparing that rate across every part, as a table and as bars. The rest stay plain, because a row naming a part has nothing to chart and a row holding a count or a capacity is not a throughput.

Numeric formats

What each matrix engine accepts as an operand, smallest first, and what it accumulates into. The accumulator is the column usually left out, and without it a narrow operand reads as narrow arithmetic: almost every engine here multiplies in 4, 6 or 8 bits and adds into 32.

Keyed by architecture rather than by card, because this is an architectural property — the three Blackwell parts answer identically, and so do the four Tenstorrent cards. A cell reads vector where the format is a general ALU type but not a matrix operand, and convert where it must be widened before the engine will take it. Both are “supported”; neither runs at matrix rate.

A format name is a link where more than one part has a published rate for it. Those pages carry two charts — the vector lanes and the matrix engine, kept apart because a lane's multiply-add and a matrix array's cell are different units of work — with dense and sparse figures on separate charts and the accumulate column carried through from this table. The formats left as plain labels are the ones no vendor publishes a comparable rate for.

Private to one column

Formats from the table above for which one architecture is the only path. A convert cell counts as a path, so a format another architecture only widens is not listed. The rows are read off those cells.

How to read these diagrams

Every page carries two views of the same part, because they answer different questions.

The die map comes first: every block on the die drawn at its own place on one grid. This is the view that shows what a spec sheet cannot — which edge the memory controllers sit on, how many cores one L2 has to serve, which column of tiles a memory channel feeds. Hover a block for its detail, use the filter chips to isolate one class, and click a block to open it below.

The hierarchy follows: the card opens into its die, the die into its clusters, and down to the thing that actually does the multiply. Click any block marked open ›, use the breadcrumb to come back out, press Esc to go up one level. Every view has its own URL, so a link can point straight at a single SIMD32 inside a compute unit.

One map is real and the rest are logical, and each page says which it is. Wormhole and Blackhole have SoC descriptors giving every tile's NOC coordinate, so those maps are the actual grid, drawn in the vendor's own coordinates. The GPU parts have block diagrams but no per-unit geometry, so those maps take the vendor's arrangement and lay it out for legibility — not to scale, and not to physical position. On a harvested part the disabled units are drawn struck through so the cut is visible at a glance; the count is real and the placement is illustrative. Each page lists its sources.
